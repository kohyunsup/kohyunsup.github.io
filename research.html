Research

International conflict does not always escalate to war. It often takes the form of hostile action below the war threshold: weapons tests, border incidents, and other provocations that impose real costs while stopping short of open conflict. My research asks how states manage this conflict in the relationship between an asymmetric alliance and its adversary. On the alliance side, I study how the two allies divide the work of handling a shared adversary, and I argue that junior allies can influence adversary behavior independently of their patron. On the adversary side, I study how the target of that management makes and communicates its own foreign policy choices. I build original measures of what each ally does toward an adversary and test the argument quantitatively, centering on the United States, South Korea, and North Korea.

Job market paper

Assurance from Junior Allies: Cooperative Management of Adversaries Below the War Threshold.
Preparing to submit to the Journal of Conflict Resolution.

Conventional wisdom on asymmetric alliances focuses on deterring war and therefore centers the senior ally's capabilities, casting junior allies as dependent. This paper examines cases where all-out war is effectively deterred but adversaries still engage in low-intensity provocations. I argue that in this space assurance rather than coercion shapes adversary behavior, and that junior allies can play an independent role. Using an original monthly dataset of U.S. and South Korean policy toward North Korea from 1991 to 2011, coded across five instruments, I find that South Korea's cooperative rhetoric and U.S. diplomatic engagement are each independently associated with fewer provocations. Managing adversaries below the war threshold depends not only on a patron's threats but on how each ally deploys specific assurance instruments.

Dissertation

The Role of Junior Allies in Alliance Management: The U.S.–ROK Alliance and North Korea. Defense expected spring 2027.

The dissertation asks a broader question than the paper: once a patron has made major war prohibitively costly, how do the two allies share the work of managing what remains? It begins from a condition I call coercion saturation. Where a patron's strategic forces already make major war prohibitively costly, additional coercive moves mostly repeat a threat the adversary understands. What remains uncertain is whether restraint will be acknowledged and reciprocated, which is a question of assurance rather than threat, and one the junior ally is structurally placed to answer.

Six chapters test that argument against different evidence. The first two develop the theory and the measurement strategy; the third is the empirical test described above. The fourth extends the data through 2024 to ask whether the pattern holds under Kim Jong Un. The fifth moves beyond the Korean triad, using event data to compare asymmetric alliances across the record. The last draws out the implications for alliance management and burden sharing.

Data

U.S. and South Korean Policy Toward North Korea, 1991–2024. An original monthly dataset hand-coded from primary government records, principally the South Korean Ministry of National Defense's defense chronology and the Ministry of Unification's chronology of inter-Korean relations and unification white papers, together with U.S. national security strategies and departmental releases. Each ally's policy is coded separately across five instruments: economic aid, diplomacy, sanctions, rhetoric, and military posture. Because the instruments are generic rather than case-specific, the coding scheme is portable to other alliance-adversary triads. Available on request.
